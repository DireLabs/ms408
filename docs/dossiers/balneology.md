## 1. Peter of Eboli's De balneis Puteolanis and its illustrated tradition

- **[C]** *De balneis Puteolanis* is a didactic Latin poem attributed to Peter of Eboli (Petrus de
  Ebulo), composed between the 1190s and c. 1220 (composition dates contested: c. 1197 under Henry VI
  vs. 1211–1220 under Frederick II; attribution also occasionally given to Alcadino di Siracusa or
  Eustazio di Matera). It celebrates the thermal baths of Pozzuoli/Baiae near Naples in ~35 verse
  epigrams (D'Amato argues 39 originally), one per bath, each naming the bath and the ailments its
  waters cure. (Wikipedia, "De balneis Puteolanis", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_balneis_Puteolanis)
- **[C]** The original manuscript is lost; counts of surviving copies vary by census — 28 copies
  (Wikipedia, ibid.) vs. Kauffmann's 20 copies of which 10 are illuminated, "each with one miniature
  for each of the 35 baths described" (C. M. Kauffmann, *The Baths of Pozzuoli: A Study of the
  Medieval Illuminations of Peter of Eboli's Poem*, Oxford: Bruno Cassirer, 1959;
  https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Baths_of_Pozzuoli.html?id=DtIIAQAAIAAJ). The discrepancy
  is a census-date artifact; treat exact counts as unresolved.
- **[C]** Key witnesses: Rome, Biblioteca Angelica Ms. 1474 (oldest extant, southern Italy/Naples,
  third quarter 13th c., 18 full-page miniatures, made for Manfred of Sicily per the facsimile
  literature — https://www.facsimilefinder.com/facsimiles/angelica-baths-pozzuoli-facsimile);
  Cologny, Cod. Bodmer 135 (mid-14th c., digitized on e-codices); Paris BnF lat. 8161; Valencia
  MS 838; Vatican Ross. 379; Edinburgh UL Ms. 176 (15th c., incomplete, crude lower-half-of-page
  illustrations — https://archives.collections.ed.ac.uk/repositories/2/archival_objects/160996).
  Almost all originate in southern Italy, 14th–15th c. (Wikipedia, ibid.)
- **[C]** A Naples Aragonese-scriptorium group of copies is documented in the scholarly literature
  (Traditio article, "Peter of Eboli, 'De Balneis Puteolanis': Manuscripts from the Aragonese
  Scriptorium in Naples",
  https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/traditio/article/abs/peter-of-eboli-de-balneis-puteolanis-manuscripts-from-the-aragonese-scriptorium-in-naples/C804287BB668512B4D019696E0B114C8).
- **[C]** The poem circulated in vernacular versions: Neapolitan dialect, French translations
  (Richart Eudes's French translation has a critical edition in *Traditio*:
  https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/traditio/article/abs/an-edition-of-richart-eudess-french-translation-of-pietro-da-ebolis-de-balneis-puteolanis/D3CC18134DE2CBA1618BB06489B320E4),
  and early printed editions. (Wikipedia, ibid.)
- **[C]** Fikret Yegül's study of the Baiae/Pozzuoli complex found that only about 7 of the 35 baths
  appear consistently across the ~10 illuminated copies known c. 1996; the Balneum Trituli was famed
  for frescoes of figures pointing at the body parts each bath cured (F. Yegül, "The Thermo-Mineral
  Complex at Baiae and De Balneis Puteolanis", *Art Bulletin* 78 (1996); summarized with the
  manuscript comparisons at J.K. Petersen, "The Baths of Puteolanis", Voynich Portal, 2017,
  https://voynichportal.com/2017/09/13/the-baths-of-puteolanis/).

## 2. Italian spa culture and balneological treatises, c. 1350–1450

The window brackets the VMS vellum radiocarbon range (1404–1438). Balneology in this period is a
live, prestigious, elite-adjacent genre of Italian academic medicine:

- **[C]** Gentile da Foligno (d. 1348) wrote a treatise on the waters of Porretta (first printed
  1473). Giovanni de' Dondi dall'Orologio (d. 1388) worked on the Abano/Padua thermal springs and
  extracted salts from them. (N. Pelling, "Q13 and Voynich balneology sources...?", Cipher Mysteries,
  2009, https://ciphermysteries.com/2009/06/18/q13-and-voynich-balneology-sources — Pelling's list
  draws on A. C. Klebs, "Balneology in the Middle Ages", 1916.)
- **[C]** Ugolino da Montecatini (c. 1345–1425), Tuscan physician, wrote the *Tractatus de balneis*
  (dated 1417 in some literature, 1419–1420 in others — flag: date unresolved), often described as
  the first systematic treatise of medical hydrology, based on waters he personally tested.
  (French Wikipedia, "Ugolino da Montecatini", https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ugolino_da_Montecatini)
- **[C]** Michele Savonarola (c. 1385–1466), professor at Padua then Ferrara court physician,
  completed *De balneis et thermis naturalibus omnibus Italiae* (a.k.a. *De balneis et termis
  Ytalie*) in 1448–49, the most comprehensive spa treatise of its time, analyzing bath types, mineral
  components, and the principal Italian springs; printed Ferrara 1485. (Library of Congress authority
  record, https://id.loc.gov/authorities/names/nr2003013895.html; critical edition project,
  Universidad de Valladolid, https://portaldelaciencia.uva.es/documentos/619ca0c2a08dbd1b8f9f10c9)
- **[C]** Other contemporaries: Francesco da Siena, *De balneis* (early Quattrocento overview of
  Italian thermalism — https://www.researchgate.net/publication/281795254); Pietro da Tussignano;
  Matteo Bendinelli on the baths of Lucca/Corsena (1489, just past our window). Tuscan spa
  infrastructure (Petriolo, Bagno a Morba, Montecatini, Porretta) and its late-medieval clientele are
  studied in D. Boisseuil, *Le thermalisme en Toscane à la fin du Moyen Âge* (École française de
  Rome, 2002; https://books.openedition.org/efr/310?lang=en).
- **[C]** The genre was consolidated in print as *De balneis omnia quae extant* (Venice: Giunta,
  1553, 447 leaves), commonly called the first balneology textbook, collecting the medieval corpus.
  (Pelling 2009, ibid.)
- **[C]** Composite balneological miscellanies circulated in the 15th c.: Pavia MS Aldini 488,
  "Collectio de Balneis" (15th-c. paper MS, 78 fols.), collects Savonarola, Ugolino da Montecatini,
  Peter of Eboli, Gentile da Foligno, and Pietro da Tussignano in one volume — direct evidence that
  a single codex mixing verse (Eboli) and prose treatises on baths is a real period object type.
  (N. Pelling, "Pavia MS Aldini 488", Cipher Mysteries, 2019,
  http://ciphermysteries.com/2019/12/01/pavia-ms-aldini-488-collectio-de-balneis)
- **[C]** The genre was not only Italian: it "flourished across the Italian, German, and Bohemian
  medical traditions throughout the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries," and Johannes Hartlieb
  (c. 1410–68) — the W4 dossier-3 figure — "wrote about plants, women, magic, astronomy and baths."
  (K. Brewer & M. L. Lewis, "The Voynich Manuscript, Dr Johannes Hartlieb and the Encipherment of
  Women's Secrets", *Social History of Medicine* 37:3 (2024), 559–582,
  https://academic.oup.com/shm/article-abstract/37/3/559/7633883; popular summary: The Conversation,
  https://theconversation.com/for-600-years-the-voynich-manuscript-has-remained-a-mystery-now-we-think-its-partly-about-sex-227157)
- **[C]** Therapeutic indications in this literature centrally include women's medicine: baths to
  warm the womb, regulate menstruation, aid postpartum recovery, and treat fluid-retention and
  reproductive complaints. (Brewer & Lewis 2024 framing, as summarized in "The medieval book nobody
  can read", The Quack Doctor, https://thequackdoctor.substack.com/p/the-medieval-book-nobody-can-read)

## 3. How bathing scenes were conventionally illustrated

- **[C]** The De balneis Puteolanis format: small text block per bath with a half-page (or, in
  Angelica 1474, full-page) miniature; nude bathers (male and female) immersed in masonry basins or
  natural pools; settings include domed bath buildings, open-air group-immersion pools, and
  underground grotto/cave sudatoria over the thermal source; ancillary elements: shore figures,
  boats, mountains, fortified towns, tents for post-treatment rest, vessels of heated water.
  (Wikipedia, "De balneis Puteolanis", ibid.; Petersen 2017, ibid.)
- **[C]** Water in the Balneis MSS is conventionally rendered green (also blue) — the same
  green-water convention as VMS Q13 pools. (R. Zandbergen, "The illustrations in the manuscript",
  voynich.nu, https://www.voynich.nu/illustr.html; Petersen 2017, ibid.)
- **[C]** Figures pointing to the afflicted body part cured by a given bath are a documented
  convention (Balneum Trituli frescoes and their manuscript echoes; Yegül 1996 via Petersen 2017).
- **[C]** Illustrated balneological texts (beyond Eboli) "show women entering baths in sequences,
  moving from pool to pool, sometimes holding implements or labels that marked the therapeutic
  stage of the treatment." (Brewer & Lewis 2024 tradition summary via search-indexed abstract/press
  coverage, ibid.) Comparable bathing figures appear in a 15th-c. Middle Dutch Trotula translation
  (The Quack Doctor, ibid.).
- **[C]** Secular Fountain-of-Youth iconography is contemporary with the VMS: the Castello della
  Manta Baronial Hall fresco (Piedmont, shortly after 1420) shows men and women undressing and
  bathing together in an arched fountain pool, with speech scrolls in Provençal French attached to
  the figures. (Wikipedia, "Castello della Manta", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castello_della_Manta;
  Web Gallery of Art, https://www.wga.hu/html_m/m/master/castell/fountai2.html) Lucas Cranach's
  *Jungbrunnen* (1546) is a later member of the same family and has been compared to VMS bathers
  (http://voynichbirths.blogspot.com/2014/05/cranach-and-voynich-manuscript.html) — the Cranach
  comparison is anachronistic for origin arguments; use only as iconographic-type evidence. **[D]**
- **[D]** Bohemian parallels (Wenceslas IV Bible bath-maiden marginalia, c. 1390s) recur in
  community discussion as a nymph-iconography comparandum but were not verified to a scholarly
  source in this session — flagged for follow-up, not asserted. (General nymph-comparison exercise:
  Koen Gheuens, "Comparing Voynich Nymphs to other Sources", 2016,
  https://herculeaf.wordpress.com/2016/10/10/proportions3/)

## 4. Textual genres attached to the bathing tradition

- **[C]** Three genre layers coexist in the tradition: (1) **verse** — Eboli's epigrams (one short
  poem per bath, panegyric + indication list; later German verse examples exist in the broader
  tradition); (2) **regimen/consilium prose** — the 14th–15th-c. academic treatises (Gentile,
  Dondi, Ugolino, Savonarola): water typology, mineral content, indications, and usage regimen
  (duration, season, diet, stages of treatment); (3) **recipe/indication lists** — per-bath ailment
  lists, the structural unit inherited from Eboli and from bath-site frescoes. (Sources as in §§1–2;
  genre synthesis is ours — the mapping of these three layers onto any VMS text structure is **[D]**.)
- **[C]** Text-image ratio in illustrated Balneis MSS is low and page-regular: short caption-like
  text per image, one bath per opening/page. (Wikipedia, ibid.; Kauffmann 1959, ibid.)

## 5. The balneological reading of VMS Quire 13 — scholarly literature

- **[C]** VMS Q13 (ff. 75r–84v, Currier B) shows dozens of unclothed female figures ("nymphs,"
  many with headdresses) standing, sitting, or immersed in green pools connected by pipes, channels,
  and tubular/organic structures. (voynich.nu, ibid.; Kennedy & Churchill 2004 description, below.)
- **[C]** The term "balneological" entered VMS studies because "several people have, independently
  from each other, pointed out a resemblance of these illustrations with the MSs of the Balneis
  Puteolanis" (voynich.nu, ibid., which prints side-by-side comparison with Vatican Ross. 379).
  No single first proposer is established in the sources consulted — flag: attribution of priority
  unresolved; earlier literature (D'Imperio 1978) and 1990s mailing-list archives should be checked
  before any priority claim is made. **[D]**
- **[C]** **Kennedy/Churchill verification (per task):** Gerry Kennedy & Rob Churchill, *The Voynich
  Manuscript* (2004; reissued Inner Traditions 2006), describe the section vividly ("plump 'nymphs'
  ... in pools of limpid green liquid; the pools ... connected by streams, channels or 'plumbing'
  that appears more organic than architectural") — but their own interpretive thesis is
  **glossolalia/channelling/outsider art**, comparing the nymphs' repetitiveness and star-streams to
  Hildegard of Bingen's migraine-influenced imagery. They are **not** the strongest statement of the
  balneological reading; they are a survey plus a psychogenic hypothesis. (Wikipedia, "Voynich
  manuscript", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voynich_manuscript; publisher page,
  https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/The-Voynich-Manuscript/Gerry-Kennedy/9781594771293)
- **[C]** The strongest sustained modern statements are instead: (1) **Nick Pelling**, *The Curse of
  the Voynich* (Compelling Press, 2006) and subsequent blog corpus — Q13 as water/balneology
  content, within his Antonio Averlino ("Filarete") authorship hypothesis (Averlino's lost "little
  books" included one on water/spas); Pelling endorses **Glen Claston's** codicological split of Q13
  into Q13A (water in general) and Q13B (thermal baths proper), with bifolios interleaved by a later
  rebinding. (Cipher Mysteries 2009, ibid.; "Voynich Averlino hypothesis summary", 2010,
  https://ciphermysteries.com/2010/01/10/voynich-averlino-hypothesis-summary) The Averlino
  attribution itself is **[D]**; the Q13A/Q13B codicology is **[C]** as a published proposal, not a
  settled fact. (2) **Brewer & Lewis 2024** (peer-reviewed) — Q13 read as balneology embedded in
  women's-secrets medicine, with the bath-sequence iconography of the genre as the operative
  parallel; this is the strongest *peer-reviewed* statement and is the bridge to W4 dossier 3.
  (Brewer & Lewis 2024, ibid.)
- **[C]** Petersen's differential assessment: VMS Q13 shares with the Balneis tradition the multiple
  pools, arches, varied blue/green water, and cavern/gallery imagery, but is "more abstract —
  combining bathing practices with anatomical structures, astrological medicine, and mythological
  elements ... rather than depicting literal bath complexes with practical architectural detail."
  (Petersen 2017, ibid.)

## 6. Constraints for W5/W6 — fit and deviation

**Where the documented tradition fits Q13 (supports "balneological milieu" as a live narrative):**

1. **[C]** Genre existence and timing: illustrated bath books with short text units per image,
   green-rendered water, nude figures in basins, and grotto/vaulted settings were in active
   circulation in Italy (and Germany/Bohemia) exactly in the vellum window (1404–1438).
2. **[C]** Composite codices mixing verse and prose bath texts (Aldini 488 type) existed — a
   multi-genre bathing quire inside a larger medical compilation is period-plausible.
3. **[C]** Women-only bathing imagery is compatible with the women's-medicine strand of the genre
   (Brewer & Lewis 2024), not an anomaly per se.
4. **[C]** Sequence semantics: the genre's "pool-to-pool in therapeutic stages, holding implements
   or labels" convention offers a concrete candidate reading for Q13's nymph chains and held objects.

**Where Q13 deviates from every documented exemplar (constraints any balneological narrative must
absorb; each deviation is also W7-relevant):**

5. **[C]** No documented Balneis MS shows engineered interconnecting plumbing between pools; VMS
   conduits are pervasive, organic-looking, and structurally load-bearing to the page design
   (Kennedy & Churchill description; Petersen 2017). The tradition depicts discrete named baths.
6. **[C]** Balneis imagery is topographically literal (named site, building type, town in corner);
   Q13 has no legible architecture, no site labels in any known script, no male bathers.
7. **[C]** Balneis text units are short captions/epigrams per image; whether Q13's text-per-image
   distribution matches that profile is measurable, not yet measured (see §7). Q13 pages are
   text-dense relative to a caption regime — if that impression survives measurement, it is a
   deviation.
8. **[D]** The astral connections (nymphs + star imagery continuity with Q13-adjacent cosmological
   pages) have no parallel in the balneological corpus consulted; a pure-balneology narrative must
   either detach those pages codicologically (Claston Q13A/Q13B) or explain the hybrid.
9. **[D]** Priority/first-proposer of the balneological reading is unresolved; W5 must not cite
   "the balneological identification" as if it were a single attributed scholarly act.
